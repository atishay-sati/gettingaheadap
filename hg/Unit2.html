Unit 2: Population and Migration Patterns and Processes

Population Distribution, Growth, Demographics, and Migration  |  Exam Weight: 12–17%

👥 Unit Overview

AP Human Geography Unit 2 focuses on where people live, why populations grow or decline, and why people move from one place to another. This unit connects population patterns to resources, culture, economics, politics, health care, and the environment.

You will study population distribution, density, fertility, mortality, population pyramids, the demographic transition model, Malthusian theory, migration patterns, push and pull factors, refugees, guest workers, and immigration policies.

Big question: How do population patterns and migration processes shape human geography across the world?

Topic 2.1 — Population Distribution

🌍 Where People Live

Population distribution means the way people are spread across Earth. People are not evenly distributed. Some areas are very crowded, while others have very few people.

Major Population Clusters

  • East Asia: China, Japan, South Korea, and nearby areas have major population concentrations.
  • South Asia: India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and surrounding areas have extremely high populations.
  • Southeast Asia: Indonesia, the Philippines, Vietnam, Thailand, and nearby areas contain large populations.
  • Europe: Western and central Europe have high population concentrations, especially around cities and industrial regions.

Why People Cluster in Certain Places

  • Fresh water: People often live near rivers, lakes, and coastlines.
  • Fertile soil: Farming is easier in areas with good soil and reliable rainfall.
  • Mild climate: Moderate climates are usually easier for settlement.
  • Economic opportunity: Jobs attract people to cities and industrial areas.
  • Transportation: Ports, rivers, roads, and railroads support trade and settlement.

Sparsely Populated Areas

  • Deserts: Limited water makes settlement harder.
  • Polar regions: Extreme cold limits farming and settlement.
  • High mountains: Steep terrain and cold temperatures make transportation and agriculture difficult.
  • Dense rainforests: Heavy rainfall, poor soil, disease, and thick vegetation can limit large settlements.
World population density — shows that people are unevenly distributed across Earth.

Topic 2.2 — Consequences of Population Distribution

📍 Why Distribution Matters

Population distribution affects political power, economic development, transportation, resource use, pollution, urban planning, and access to services. Where people live influences how governments and societies organize space.

Population Density Types

  • Arithmetic density: Total population divided by total land area.
  • Physiological density: Total population divided by arable land, or land that can be farmed.
  • Agricultural density: Number of farmers divided by arable land.

Why Density Types Matter

  • Arithmetic density gives a general sense of crowding but can hide uneven settlement.
  • Physiological density helps show pressure on farmland and food production.
  • Agricultural density can show how developed or mechanized a farming system is.

Consequences of High Population Density

  • More pressure on housing, roads, schools, hospitals, and public transportation.
  • More pollution and waste if infrastructure cannot keep up.
  • Greater demand for food, water, energy, and jobs.
  • More cultural interaction, innovation, and economic activity in cities.

Topic 2.3 — Population Composition

📊 Who Makes Up a Population?

Population composition means the structure of a population, including age, sex, gender, occupation, education, and other demographic traits. One of the most important tools for this topic is the population pyramid.

Population Pyramids

  • Population pyramid: A graph that shows the age and sex structure of a population.
  • Wide base: Usually shows high birth rates and many young people.
  • Narrow base: Usually shows low birth rates and slower population growth.
  • Large older population: Suggests aging, longer life expectancy, and possible future labor shortages.

Important Population Groups

  • Dependency ratio: Compares dependents, young and old, to the working-age population.
  • Youth dependency: A large number of children compared to workers.
  • Elderly dependency: A large number of older adults compared to workers.
  • Sex ratio: Number of males compared to females in a population.

Why Population Composition Matters

  • A young population needs schools, childcare, pediatric health care, and future jobs.
  • An aging population needs retirement support, health care, elder care, and a strong workforce.
  • A high dependency ratio can put pressure on working adults and government programs.
Population pyramid — shows age and sex structure, which helps predict future population needs.

Topic 2.4 — Population Dynamics

📈 Births, Deaths, and Population Change

Population dynamics refers to how and why populations grow, shrink, or change over time. Geographers study births, deaths, migration, fertility, mortality, and life expectancy.

Key Measures

  • Crude birth rate: Number of births per 1,000 people in a year.
  • Crude death rate: Number of deaths per 1,000 people in a year.
  • Rate of natural increase: Birth rate minus death rate, not including migration.
  • Total fertility rate: Average number of children a woman is expected to have in her lifetime.
  • Infant mortality rate: Number of infant deaths per 1,000 live births.
  • Life expectancy: Average number of years a person is expected to live.

High Fertility Usually Connects To

  • Limited access to education, especially for women and girls.
  • Limited access to health care or family planning.
  • Need for children to help with farming or family labor.
  • Cultural or religious values that support larger families.
  • High infant mortality, which can lead families to have more children.

Low Fertility Usually Connects To

  • Higher education levels and more career opportunities.
  • Urbanization and higher cost of raising children.
  • Better access to health care and family planning.
  • Later marriage and delayed childbearing.
  • Economic development and changing social expectations.

Topic 2.5 — The Demographic Transition Model

🚼 How Population Changes as Countries Develop

The demographic transition model, or DTM, explains how birth rates, death rates, and population growth usually change as a country industrializes and develops.

Stage 1: Low Growth

  • High birth rate and high death rate.
  • Population grows slowly or stays stable.
  • Usually connected to preindustrial societies.
  • Death rates are high because of disease, famine, poor sanitation, and limited medicine.

Stage 2: Rapid Growth

  • Death rate drops quickly, but birth rate remains high.
  • Population grows rapidly.
  • Death rates decline because of better food supply, sanitation, medicine, and public health.
  • Many developing countries have experienced this stage.

Stage 3: Slowing Growth

  • Birth rate begins to fall.
  • Death rate stays low.
  • Population still grows, but more slowly.
  • Birth rates decline because of urbanization, education, family planning, and economic change.

Stage 4: Low Growth

  • Low birth rate and low death rate.
  • Population is stable or grows slowly.
  • Common in highly developed countries.
  • Families often have fewer children due to high costs, education, and career opportunities.

Stage 5: Decline

  • Birth rate falls below death rate.
  • Population may shrink over time.
  • Common concerns include aging population, labor shortages, and pension stress.
  • Some countries respond with pronatalist policies or immigration incentives.
Demographic Transition Model — explains how birth and death rates change with development.

Topic 2.6 — Malthusian Theory

🌾 Population Growth and Food Supply

Thomas Malthus argued that population grows faster than food supply. He believed population could grow exponentially, while food supply would grow more slowly. Because of this, he predicted famine, disease, and conflict would limit population growth.

Malthusian Ideas

  • Population growth: Malthus believed population could grow very quickly if unchecked.
  • Food supply: He believed food production would not keep up with population growth.
  • Positive checks: Events that increase death rates, such as famine, disease, or war.
  • Preventive checks: Actions that reduce birth rates, such as delayed marriage or having fewer children.

Criticism of Malthus

  • He did not predict major advances in agricultural technology.
  • He underestimated global trade and food distribution improvements.
  • He did not fully account for falling birth rates as countries develop.
  • Some hunger problems are caused by poverty, inequality, conflict, or distribution, not only lack of food.

Neo-Malthusian View

Neo-Malthusians argue that population growth can still create problems, especially when linked to limited resources, environmental damage, water shortages, and climate stress.

Topic 2.7 — Population Policies

🏛️ How Governments Influence Population

Governments sometimes try to influence population growth. Some countries want to increase birth rates, while others want to slow growth or manage migration.

Pronatalist Policies

  • Goal: Encourage people to have more children.
  • Common in: Countries with low birth rates or aging populations.
  • Examples: Paid parental leave, child tax credits, childcare support, housing benefits, or baby bonuses.
  • Reason: Governments may worry about labor shortages, aging populations, and shrinking tax bases.

Antinatalist Policies

  • Goal: Encourage people to have fewer children.
  • Common in: Countries worried about rapid population growth and pressure on resources.
  • Examples: Family planning programs, education, access to contraception, or incentives for smaller families.
  • Reason: Governments may worry about food, housing, schools, jobs, and environmental stress.

Women and Population Change

Education and empowerment of women are strongly connected to lower fertility rates. When women have more access to schooling, employment, health care, and political rights, family size often decreases.

Topic 2.8 — Migration Patterns

🚶 Why People Move

Migration is the movement of people from one place to another. Migration can be voluntary or forced, internal or international, permanent or temporary.

Types of Migration

  • Internal migration: Movement within one country.
  • International migration: Movement from one country to another.
  • Voluntary migration: People choose to move, usually for jobs, education, family, or lifestyle.
  • Forced migration: People are forced to move because of war, persecution, slavery, disaster, or violence.
  • Chain migration: People move to a place where family or community members already live.
  • Step migration: Movement in stages, such as village to town to city to another country.
  • Return migration: Migrants move back to their place of origin.

Common Migration Patterns

  • Rural-to-urban migration: People move from countryside areas to cities for jobs and services.
  • Urban-to-suburban migration: People move from cities to suburbs, often for housing or schools.
  • Guest worker migration: Workers move temporarily for jobs, often in construction, agriculture, or service industries.
  • Refugee migration: People flee danger and cross borders seeking safety.
Refugee camp — connects to forced migration, political instability, and refugee movement.

Topic 2.9 — Push and Pull Factors

🧲 What Pushes and Pulls Migrants?

Push and pull factors explain why people leave one place and choose another. A push factor drives people away, while a pull factor attracts people to a new location.

Push Factors

  • Economic: Unemployment, low wages, poverty, or lack of land.
  • Political: War, persecution, corruption, or lack of freedom.
  • Environmental: Drought, flooding, earthquakes, desertification, or climate-related problems.
  • Social: Discrimination, limited education, poor health care, or family separation.

Pull Factors

  • Economic: Better jobs, higher wages, business opportunities, or available land.
  • Political: Safety, stability, rights, and protection.
  • Environmental: Better climate, safer housing, or more reliable resources.
  • Social: Family connections, education, health care, and community networks.

Intervening Obstacles

Intervening obstacles are barriers that make migration harder. These can include distance, cost, mountains, oceans, laws, borders, language, discrimination, paperwork, or lack of transportation.

Topic 2.10 — Effects of Migration

🌐 Migration Changes Both Places

Migration affects both the place people leave and the place they enter. These effects can be economic, cultural, political, and demographic.

Effects on Sending Countries

  • Remittances: Money migrants send back home to family members.
  • Brain drain: Loss of educated or skilled workers from a country.
  • Reduced labor pressure: Fewer workers competing for jobs locally.
  • Family separation: Migration can separate families across borders.

Effects on Receiving Countries

  • Labor supply: Migrants may fill jobs in agriculture, construction, health care, services, and technology.
  • Cultural diversity: Migration can bring new languages, foods, religions, and traditions.
  • Political debate: Immigration can create debates about citizenship, borders, jobs, and national identity.
  • Urban growth: Migrants often settle in cities, increasing demand for housing and services.

Assimilation and Multiculturalism

  • Assimilation: Migrants adopt many cultural traits of the receiving society.
  • Multiculturalism: Different cultural groups keep distinct traditions while living in the same society.
  • Ethnic enclaves: Neighborhoods with a concentration of people from the same cultural background.

📝 Multiple Choice Practice

Click an answer to check it. Each question includes an explanation.

1. Which measure best shows pressure on farmland?

(A) Arithmetic density
(B) Physiological density
(C) Sex ratio
(D) Life expectancy
Answer: B. Physiological density compares population to arable land, so it shows pressure on farmland.

2. A population pyramid with a very wide base usually indicates which pattern?

(A) Low birth rates and an aging population
(B) High birth rates and a young population
(C) No population growth
(D) A high elderly dependency ratio
Answer: B. A wide base means many children are being born, which usually shows high birth rates.

3. In Stage 2 of the demographic transition model, why does population grow rapidly?

(A) Birth rates and death rates are both low.
(B) Birth rates fall below death rates.
(C) Death rates fall while birth rates remain high.
(D) Migration stops completely.
Answer: C. In Stage 2, better sanitation, medicine, and food lower death rates, but birth rates are still high.

4. Which is an example of a pull factor?

(A) War in a home country
(B) Drought destroying crops
(C) Better job opportunities in another country
(D) Political persecution
Answer: C. A pull factor attracts people to a place, and better jobs are a common pull factor.

5. What are remittances?

(A) Taxes paid by migrants to a receiving country
(B) Money migrants send back to family in their home country
(C) Laws that prevent migration
(D) Maps showing population density
Answer: B. Remittances are money sent by migrants to family or communities in their place of origin.

⭐ Key Takeaways

  • Population is unevenly distributed. People cluster near water, fertile land, cities, coasts, and economic opportunities.
  • Density has different meanings. Arithmetic, physiological, and agricultural density each reveal different things.
  • Population pyramids predict future needs. They show age structure, birth rates, aging, and dependency problems.
  • The DTM explains population change. Birth and death rates shift as societies develop.
  • Malthus warned about population pressure. His theory is important, but technology and development changed parts of the prediction.
  • Population policies try to influence birth rates. Pronatalist policies encourage births, while antinatalist policies discourage them.
  • Migration is shaped by push and pull factors. People move because of jobs, safety, family, war, environment, and opportunity.
  • Migration affects both sending and receiving places. It can create remittances, brain drain, labor supply, cultural diversity, and political debate.
