Unit 5: Agriculture and Rural Land-Use Patterns and Processes

Farming, Food Production, Rural Settlement, and Land Use  |  Exam Weight: 12–17%

🌾 Unit Overview

AP Human Geography Unit 5 focuses on agriculture, food production, rural settlement, and how humans use land to grow crops and raise animals. Agriculture is one of the most important human activities because it shapes landscapes, economies, cultures, diets, settlement patterns, and the environment.

In this unit, you will study the origins of agriculture, agricultural hearths, diffusion, rural settlement patterns, agricultural revolutions, commercial and subsistence farming, the von Thünen model, agribusiness, food security, sustainability, biotechnology, and the environmental consequences of farming.

Big question: How do agriculture and food production shape rural landscapes, economies, and human-environment interactions?

Topic 5.1 — Introduction to Agriculture

🌱 What Is Agriculture?

Agriculture is the practice of growing crops and raising animals for food, fiber, fuel, and other products. It changed human history because it allowed people to settle in one place, produce food surpluses, build villages, specialize in different jobs, and develop complex societies.

Basic Agriculture Terms

  • Domestication: The process of adapting plants or animals for human use.
  • Crop: A plant grown by humans for food or other uses.
  • Livestock: Animals raised for food, labor, or products such as milk, wool, or leather.
  • Subsistence agriculture: Farming mainly to feed the farmer’s family or local community.
  • Commercial agriculture: Farming mainly to sell products for profit.
  • Intensive agriculture: Farming that uses a lot of labor or inputs on a small amount of land.
  • Extensive agriculture: Farming that uses large amounts of land with fewer inputs per acre.

Why Agriculture Matters

  • It supports population growth by creating more stable food supplies.
  • It creates rural landscapes such as fields, villages, terraces, barns, canals, and plantations.
  • It affects culture through diets, festivals, land ownership, gender roles, and traditions.
  • It creates environmental impacts such as deforestation, soil erosion, irrigation, pollution, and habitat loss.

Topic 5.2 — Settlement Patterns and Survey Methods

🏘️ Rural Settlement Patterns

Rural settlement patterns show how homes, farms, roads, and fields are arranged in agricultural areas. These patterns are shaped by physical geography, culture, land ownership, transportation, and government policies.

Settlement Patterns

  • Clustered settlement: Homes are grouped together, often near a village center, church, water source, or road.
  • Dispersed settlement: Homes are spread out across farmland, with each family living near its fields.
  • Linear settlement: Homes and farms are arranged in a line, often along a road, river, canal, or coastline.

Survey Systems

  • Metes and bounds: Uses natural features and landmarks to define property boundaries. It can create irregular property shapes.
  • Township and range: Uses a grid system to divide land into square sections. It is common in much of the United States Midwest and West.
  • Long-lot survey: Creates long, narrow farms with access to a river, road, or canal. It was common in French-settled areas.

Why Settlement Patterns Matter

Settlement patterns affect transportation, community life, access to services, land ownership, and agricultural efficiency. They also create visible cultural landscapes that geographers can study from maps or aerial images.

Township and range — a grid-based land survey system that shaped rural settlement in the United States.

Topic 5.3 — Agricultural Origins and Diffusion

🌍 Where Agriculture Began

Agriculture developed independently in several hearths around the world. From these hearths, crops, animals, tools, and farming techniques spread through migration, trade, conquest, and cultural diffusion.

Major Agricultural Hearths

  • Southwest Asia: Wheat, barley, lentils, sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs.
  • East Asia: Rice, millet, soybeans, pigs, and chickens.
  • South Asia: Rice, cotton, and other tropical crops.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa: Sorghum, yams, millet, coffee, and cattle in some regions.
  • Mesoamerica: Maize, beans, squash, peppers, and turkeys.
  • Andes region: Potatoes, quinoa, llamas, and alpacas.

First Agricultural Revolution

  • Also called the Neolithic Revolution.
  • People shifted from hunting and gathering to farming and herding.
  • Permanent settlements became more common.
  • Food surpluses allowed job specialization, trade, social hierarchy, and early states.

Diffusion of Agriculture

  • Relocation diffusion: Farmers moved and brought crops, animals, and techniques with them.
  • Expansion diffusion: Farming practices spread outward from agricultural hearths.
  • Columbian Exchange: Crops and animals moved between the Americas, Europe, Africa, and Asia after 1492.
Agricultural hearths — shows where farming began and diffused to other regions.

Topic 5.4 — The Second Agricultural Revolution

🚜 Agriculture Becomes More Productive

The Second Agricultural Revolution happened mainly in Europe during the 1700s and 1800s. It increased food production and helped support population growth and industrialization.

Important Changes

  • Crop rotation: Farmers rotated crops to maintain soil fertility and reduce fallow land.
  • Seed drill: Planted seeds more efficiently and evenly.
  • Selective breeding: Farmers bred animals and plants for better traits.
  • Enclosure movement: Common lands were fenced into private farms, making farming more efficient but displacing many peasants.
  • Mechanization: Machines reduced labor needs and increased output.

Effects

  • Food production increased.
  • Population growth accelerated.
  • Fewer farm workers were needed, causing many people to move to cities.
  • Industrialization became easier because cities had more workers and food supplies.
  • Rural landscapes changed as fields were enclosed, reorganized, and commercialized.

Topic 5.5 — The Green Revolution

🌾 High-Yield Agriculture

The Green Revolution began in the mid-1900s and used science and technology to increase crop yields, especially in developing countries. It focused heavily on crops such as wheat, rice, and maize.

Main Features

  • High-yield seeds: New varieties produced more food per acre.
  • Irrigation: More reliable water supplies increased productivity.
  • Synthetic fertilizers: Added nutrients to soil to raise yields.
  • Pesticides and herbicides: Reduced crop loss from insects and weeds.
  • Mechanization: Tractors and machines increased efficiency.

Benefits

  • Food production increased dramatically in many regions.
  • Some countries reduced famine risk and became more food secure.
  • Farmers could grow more crops on the same amount of land.
  • Urban populations could grow because food supplies became more reliable.

Criticisms and Problems

  • Fertilizers and pesticides polluted soil and water.
  • Irrigation increased pressure on rivers, aquifers, and groundwater.
  • Small farmers sometimes could not afford seeds, chemicals, and machines.
  • Biodiversity declined as farmers relied on fewer crop varieties.
  • Some regions benefited more than others, increasing inequality.

Topic 5.6 — Agricultural Production Regions

🌎 Different Places, Different Farming

Agricultural production varies by climate, soil, culture, technology, development level, labor costs, land availability, and access to markets.

Subsistence Agriculture

  • Farmers produce mainly enough food for themselves and their families.
  • Often found in developing regions.
  • Usually involves more human labor and fewer machines.
  • Examples include shifting cultivation, pastoral nomadism, and intensive subsistence rice farming.

Commercial Agriculture

  • Farmers produce crops or animals mainly for sale.
  • Often found in developed regions.
  • Usually involves machinery, science, transportation, and global markets.
  • Examples include grain farming, dairy farming, mixed crop and livestock farming, ranching, and plantation agriculture.

Major Farming Types

  • Shifting cultivation: Farmers clear land, farm it temporarily, then move when soil fertility declines.
  • Pastoral nomadism: Herding animals and moving with them to find pasture and water.
  • Intensive subsistence agriculture: Small farms with high labor input, often in densely populated regions.
  • Dairy farming: Milk production, usually near markets because milk spoils quickly.
  • Grain farming: Large-scale wheat, corn, or other grain production for sale.
  • Ranching: Raising livestock over large areas of land.
  • Plantation agriculture: Large farms growing cash crops such as coffee, sugar, cocoa, bananas, or rubber.
  • Mediterranean agriculture: Grapes, olives, citrus, vegetables, and wine production in Mediterranean climates.

Topic 5.7 — Spatial Organization of Agriculture

📍 Why Farming Happens Where It Does

Agricultural land use is spatially organized. Farmers choose what to grow based on climate, land cost, transportation cost, labor, technology, markets, government policy, and cultural preferences.

Factors That Shape Agricultural Location

  • Climate: Temperature and rainfall determine which crops can grow.
  • Soil: Fertile soil supports higher yields.
  • Land cost: Expensive land near cities encourages intensive farming.
  • Transportation: Perishable goods need fast access to markets.
  • Labor: Some crops require more workers than others.
  • Technology: Machines, refrigeration, irrigation, and fertilizers expand farming possibilities.
  • Government policy: Subsidies, tariffs, land reform, and regulations shape farming decisions.

Perishability and Market Access

Perishable products such as milk, fruit, vegetables, and flowers often need to be produced closer to markets or transported quickly using refrigeration. Less perishable products such as grain can be grown farther from markets.

Topic 5.8 — Von Thünen Model

⭕ Agricultural Land Use Around a Market

The von Thünen model explains how agricultural land use may be arranged around a central market city. It assumes flat land, one market, equal soil quality, and transportation costs based mainly on distance.

Basic Rings of the Model

  • Center: Market city where products are sold.
  • Ring 1: Dairy and market gardening because these products are perishable and land near the city is expensive.
  • Ring 2: Forest products because wood is heavy and was historically expensive to transport.
  • Ring 3: Grains and field crops because they are less perishable and can be grown farther away.
  • Ring 4: Ranching and livestock because animals need lots of land and can move to market.

Why the Model Matters

  • It shows the relationship between land cost, transportation cost, and agricultural choices.
  • It helps explain why intensive agriculture is often closer to cities.
  • It shows why extensive agriculture often occurs farther from markets.

Limitations

  • Real landscapes are not perfectly flat.
  • There are usually multiple markets, not just one.
  • Rivers, highways, railroads, ports, and refrigeration change transportation costs.
  • Government policies, technology, climate, and global trade can change land use.
Von Thünen model — explains agricultural land use based on distance from a market and transportation costs.

Topic 5.9 — The Global System of Agriculture

🌐 Food Is Part of a Global Economy

Modern agriculture is connected to global trade, multinational corporations, transportation networks, food processing, technology, and consumer demand.

Global Agriculture Concepts

  • Agribusiness: Commercial agriculture organized as a large-scale business, including production, processing, transportation, and sales.
  • Commodity chain: The full process of producing, processing, distributing, and selling a product.
  • Export commodity: A crop grown mainly to sell to other countries.
  • Cash crop: A crop grown for sale rather than direct consumption by the farmer.
  • Food desert: An area with limited access to affordable, healthy food.
  • Food security: Reliable access to enough safe and nutritious food.
  • Food insecurity: Lack of reliable access to enough food.

Global Food Trade

  • Some countries export large amounts of grain, meat, coffee, sugar, cocoa, fruit, or vegetables.
  • Some developing countries rely on export crops, which can make them vulnerable to global price changes.
  • Food travels long distances because of refrigeration, container shipping, airplanes, trucks, and global markets.
  • Consumer demand in wealthy countries can shape farming patterns in poorer countries.

Topic 5.10 — Consequences of Agricultural Practices

⚠️ Environmental and Social Effects

Agriculture feeds people, but it also changes the environment. Farming can cause serious environmental problems if land, water, soil, and chemicals are not managed carefully.

Environmental Consequences

  • Deforestation: Forests are cleared for fields, ranches, plantations, or settlement.
  • Soil erosion: Fertile topsoil is lost through wind, water, or poor farming methods.
  • Desertification: Land becomes more desert-like because of overgrazing, deforestation, drought, or poor land use.
  • Water pollution: Fertilizers, pesticides, and animal waste can runoff into rivers and lakes.
  • Salinization: Salt builds up in soil because of irrigation, making farming harder.
  • Biodiversity loss: Monocropping and habitat destruction reduce plant and animal diversity.
  • Greenhouse gases: Livestock, rice paddies, fertilizer use, and transportation contribute to emissions.

Social and Economic Consequences

  • Small farmers may struggle to compete with large agribusinesses.
  • Farmworkers may face low wages, seasonal work, and difficult working conditions.
  • Export crop dependence can make countries vulnerable to global price changes.
  • Land ownership inequality can create poverty and rural conflict.

Topic 5.11 — Challenges of Contemporary Agriculture

🔬 Modern Farming Debates

Contemporary agriculture faces challenges involving food supply, environmental sustainability, technology, inequality, climate change, and consumer choices.

Biotechnology and GMOs

  • Biotechnology: Using science to modify plants or animals for human purposes.
  • GMOs: Genetically modified organisms designed for traits such as pest resistance, drought tolerance, or higher yields.
  • Benefits: GMOs can increase yields, reduce crop loss, and help crops survive difficult conditions.
  • Concerns: Critics worry about corporate control, environmental impacts, seed costs, and biodiversity loss.

Sustainable Agriculture

  • Goal: Produce food while protecting land, water, ecosystems, and future farming ability.
  • Crop rotation: Rotating crops to protect soil nutrients.
  • Organic farming: Farming with limited synthetic chemicals.
  • Conservation tillage: Reducing plowing to prevent erosion.
  • Integrated pest management: Using multiple strategies to control pests with fewer chemicals.
  • Local food systems: Producing and buying food closer to where people live.

Climate Change and Agriculture

  • Changing rainfall patterns can increase droughts or floods.
  • Rising temperatures can reduce yields in some regions.
  • Farmers may need new crops, irrigation systems, or farming locations.
  • Food prices may rise when climate events damage crops.

Topic 5.12 — Women in Agriculture

👩‍🌾 Gender and Farming

Women play a major role in agriculture around the world, especially in subsistence farming and small-scale food production. However, women often have less access to land ownership, credit, education, equipment, and political power.

Challenges Women Farmers May Face

  • Limited legal rights to own or inherit land.
  • Less access to loans, machinery, fertilizer, and technology.
  • Less access to formal agricultural training or education.
  • Unpaid labor and household responsibilities in addition to farm work.
  • Lower wages in agricultural labor markets.

Why Gender Equity Matters

  • Improving women’s access to land and resources can increase food production.
  • Educating women often improves family health, nutrition, and income.
  • Supporting women farmers can reduce poverty and improve food security.

📝 Multiple Choice Practice

Click an answer to check it. Each question includes an explanation.

1. Which development best describes the First Agricultural Revolution?

(A) Humans shifted from hunting and gathering to farming and herding.
(B) Farmers began using high-yield seeds and synthetic fertilizers.
(C) Commercial agribusiness replaced all family farms.
(D) The von Thünen model was created.
Answer: A. The First Agricultural Revolution was the shift from hunting and gathering to plant and animal domestication.

2. Which type of agriculture is mainly practiced to feed the farmer’s family?

(A) Commercial agriculture
(B) Subsistence agriculture
(C) Plantation agriculture
(D) Agribusiness
Answer: B. Subsistence agriculture is farming mainly to feed the farmer’s family or local community.

3. In the von Thünen model, why are dairy and market gardening closest to the city?

(A) They require the least valuable land.
(B) They are not affected by transportation costs.
(C) Their products are perishable and need quick access to markets.
(D) They require the largest amount of open land.
Answer: C. Dairy and market gardening are close to the market because products like milk, vegetables, and flowers spoil quickly.

4. Which was a major feature of the Green Revolution?

(A) A return to hunting and gathering
(B) The end of global food trade
(C) High-yield seeds, irrigation, fertilizers, and pesticides
(D) The elimination of all environmental problems in farming
Answer: C. The Green Revolution increased yields through high-yield seeds, irrigation, fertilizers, pesticides, and mechanization.

5. Which problem is most directly caused by irrigation in dry regions?

(A) Hierarchical diffusion
(B) Gerrymandering
(C) Salinization
(D) Lingua franca
Answer: C. Irrigation in dry regions can leave salts behind in the soil, causing salinization.

⭐ Key Takeaways

  • Agriculture changed human society. Farming allowed permanent settlements, food surpluses, population growth, and complex societies.
  • Agriculture began in multiple hearths. Crops and animals were domesticated independently in several world regions.
  • Rural landscapes show settlement patterns. Clustered, dispersed, linear, township and range, metes and bounds, and long-lot systems shape land use.
  • Agricultural revolutions increased productivity. The First, Second, and Green Revolutions each changed food production in major ways.
  • Subsistence and commercial agriculture are different. Subsistence farming feeds local families; commercial farming produces for markets.
  • The von Thünen model explains agricultural location. Land cost, transportation cost, perishability, and market access shape farming patterns.
  • Modern agriculture is globalized. Agribusiness, commodity chains, exports, and multinational corporations shape food systems.
  • Agriculture creates environmental challenges. Deforestation, soil erosion, salinization, water pollution, biodiversity loss, and greenhouse gases are major issues.
  • Sustainable agriculture tries to protect the future. It focuses on producing food while protecting soil, water, ecosystems, and rural communities.
