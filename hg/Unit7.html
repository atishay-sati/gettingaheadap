Unit 7: Industrial and Economic Development Patterns and Processes

Industrialization, Development, Trade, Globalization, and Sustainability  |  Exam Weight: 12–17%

🏭 Unit Overview

AP Human Geography Unit 7 focuses on how industrialization and economic development shape the world. This unit explains why some places industrialized earlier than others, why economic growth is uneven, how global trade connects regions, and how development affects people, jobs, environments, and standards of living.

In this unit, you will study the Industrial Revolution, economic sectors, formal and informal economies, measures of development, gender and development, Rostow’s model, dependency theory, world-systems theory, trade, global supply chains, outsourcing, offshoring, deindustrialization, special economic zones, and sustainable development.

Big question: Why has industrialization improved living standards in some places while also creating uneven development across the world?

Topic 7.1 — The Industrial Revolution

⚙️ How Industrialization Changed the World

The Industrial Revolution was a major shift from hand production and small workshops to machine-powered factory production. It began in Great Britain in the late 1700s and later spread to other parts of Europe, North America, Japan, and eventually many other regions.

Why Britain Industrialized First

  • Coal and iron: Britain had important natural resources needed for machines, factories, and railroads.
  • Capital: Wealth from trade, banking, and empire helped fund factories and infrastructure.
  • Labor supply: Agricultural changes pushed many rural workers toward cities and factory jobs.
  • Transportation: Rivers, canals, ports, and later railroads helped move raw materials and finished goods.
  • Markets: Britain had domestic and overseas markets where manufactured goods could be sold.
  • Technology: Steam engines, textile machines, and improved metalworking increased production.

Effects of the Industrial Revolution

  • Factory production increased the amount of goods people could make.
  • Cities grew rapidly as workers moved from rural areas to industrial centers.
  • Railroads, steamships, canals, and ports expanded transportation networks.
  • New industrial regions developed near coal, iron, ports, rivers, and labor supplies.
  • Industrialization increased wealth but also created pollution, crowded cities, labor conflicts, and inequality.

Industrial Location Factors

  • Raw materials: Factories often locate near resources such as coal, iron, timber, or minerals.
  • Energy: Access to power sources influences factory location.
  • Labor: Industries need workers with the right skills and wages.
  • Transportation: Ports, roads, railroads, airports, and highways reduce shipping costs.
  • Markets: Some industries locate near customers to reduce delivery time or transportation costs.
  • Government policy: Taxes, subsidies, trade rules, and regulations influence where firms locate.

Topic 7.2 — Economic Sectors and Patterns

💼 Types of Economic Activity

Economic sectors describe different kinds of jobs and production. As countries develop, their economies often shift from primary-sector jobs toward secondary, tertiary, quaternary, and quinary activities.

The Five Economic Sectors

  • Primary sector: Extracting raw materials, such as farming, fishing, mining, forestry, and ranching.
  • Secondary sector: Manufacturing and processing, such as factories, construction, steel, textiles, and car production.
  • Tertiary sector: Services, such as retail, transportation, restaurants, health care, tourism, and banking.
  • Quaternary sector: Knowledge-based work, such as research, data analysis, education, software, and information technology.
  • Quinary sector: High-level decision-making, such as corporate leadership, government leadership, university leadership, and top scientific work.

Sector Patterns by Development Level

  • Less developed economies often have a larger percentage of workers in the primary sector.
  • Industrializing economies often grow in the secondary sector as factories expand.
  • Highly developed economies often have large tertiary, quaternary, and quinary sectors.
  • Economic change can create new jobs but can also eliminate older jobs through automation, outsourcing, or deindustrialization.

Formal and Informal Economies

  • Formal economy: Legal, taxed, and regulated economic activity.
  • Informal economy: Economic activity that is not fully regulated, taxed, or officially counted.
  • Informal jobs may include street vending, day labor, small repair work, or unregistered services.
  • The informal economy can provide income, but workers may lack job security, benefits, legal protection, or stable wages.

Topic 7.3 — Measures of Development

📊 How Development Is Measured

Development means improvement in social, economic, and political conditions. It is not just about money. A country can have economic growth but still struggle with inequality, poor health care, limited education, or environmental damage.

Economic Measures

  • GDP: Gross domestic product; the total value of goods and services produced within a country.
  • GNI: Gross national income; income earned by a country’s people and businesses, including income from abroad.
  • GDP or GNI per capita: GDP or GNI divided by population; used to estimate average income.
  • Purchasing power parity: Adjusts income to account for differences in cost of living between countries.
  • Employment structure: Shows how workers are distributed across economic sectors.

Social Measures

  • Literacy rate: Percentage of people who can read and write.
  • Life expectancy: Average number of years a person is expected to live.
  • Infant mortality rate: Number of infant deaths per 1,000 live births.
  • Education levels: School enrollment, years of schooling, and access to higher education.
  • Access to services: Health care, clean water, sanitation, electricity, internet, and transportation.

Composite Measures

  • HDI: Human Development Index combines life expectancy, education, and income.
  • GII: Gender Inequality Index measures gender-based inequality in health, empowerment, and labor force participation.
  • Poverty rate: Percentage of people living below a defined income level.
  • GINI coefficient: Measures income inequality within a country.

Limitations of Development Data

  • Averages can hide inequality within a country.
  • National data may not show differences between regions, cities, or rural areas.
  • GDP can rise even if pollution, inequality, or poor working conditions increase.
  • Some informal economic activity is not counted accurately.
  • Different governments and organizations may collect data differently.

Topic 7.4 — Women and Economic Development

👩‍💼 Gender Equity and Development

Women’s access to education, health care, employment, land ownership, political power, and financial resources strongly affects economic development. When women have more opportunities, families and countries often experience better health, higher incomes, lower fertility rates, and stronger economic growth.

Barriers Women May Face

  • Limited access to schooling or job training.
  • Lower wages for similar work.
  • Limited rights to own land, inherit property, or access credit.
  • Social expectations that limit employment or leadership opportunities.
  • Less political representation and decision-making power.
  • Unpaid household labor and caregiving responsibilities.

How Women’s Empowerment Supports Development

  • Educated women often have more employment options and higher incomes.
  • Women’s education is often connected to lower infant mortality and better family health.
  • Access to family planning can lower fertility rates and improve economic stability.
  • Women entrepreneurs can create jobs and support local economies.
  • Political participation can help shape policies on health, education, labor, and family welfare.

Policies That Can Improve Gender Equity

  • Expanding access to education for girls and women.
  • Protecting equal pay and workplace rights.
  • Improving access to health care and family planning.
  • Allowing women to own land, open bank accounts, and receive loans.
  • Supporting childcare, parental leave, and safe transportation.

Topic 7.5 — Theories of Development

🧠 Explaining Uneven Development

Development theories try to explain why some countries are wealthier, more industrialized, or more connected to the global economy than others. No theory explains everything perfectly, but each helps geographers analyze patterns of growth and inequality.

Rostow’s Stages of Economic Growth

  • Stage 1 — Traditional society: Economy is mostly subsistence agriculture with limited technology.
  • Stage 2 — Preconditions for takeoff: Infrastructure, investment, education, and technology begin improving.
  • Stage 3 — Takeoff: Industrialization increases quickly, and manufacturing expands.
  • Stage 4 — Drive to maturity: The economy diversifies, technology spreads, and industries become more advanced.
  • Stage 5 — High mass consumption: Consumer goods and services become widespread.

Strengths and Weaknesses of Rostow

  • Strength: It shows development as a process that changes over time.
  • Strength: It connects industrialization, infrastructure, investment, and consumer growth.
  • Weakness: It assumes all countries follow a similar path.
  • Weakness: It does not fully explain colonialism, dependency, inequality, or environmental limits.

Dependency Theory

  • Dependency theory argues that poor countries are kept dependent on wealthy countries through trade, debt, colonial history, and unequal economic relationships.
  • It emphasizes that development in some places can be connected to exploitation or underdevelopment in others.
  • It focuses on raw material exports, low wages, unequal trade, and outside control by powerful countries or corporations.

World-Systems Theory

  • Core countries: Wealthy, industrialized countries with advanced services, technology, and political power.
  • Semi-periphery countries: Countries with some industrial growth that are between core and periphery.
  • Periphery countries: Countries often dependent on raw material exports, low-wage labor, and less political power.
  • This theory explains global inequality as part of a connected world economic system.

Commodity Dependence

Commodity dependence happens when a country relies heavily on exporting one or a few raw materials or agricultural products. This can be risky because prices can change quickly, leaving the country vulnerable to global market shifts.

Topic 7.6 — Trade and the World Economy

🚢 Global Trade and Economic Connections

Trade connects countries through the exchange of goods, services, resources, labor, technology, and capital. Modern trade is shaped by transportation, container shipping, communication networks, trade agreements, multinational corporations, tariffs, and global supply chains.

Trade Concepts

  • Export: A good or service sold to another country.
  • Import: A good or service bought from another country.
  • Balance of trade: Difference between the value of exports and imports.
  • Comparative advantage: A country specializes in producing goods it can make relatively efficiently.
  • Free trade: Trade with fewer barriers such as tariffs or quotas.
  • Fair trade: Trade system meant to provide better wages, safer conditions, and more stable incomes for producers.

Trade Barriers and Agreements

  • Tariff: A tax on imported goods.
  • Quota: A limit on the amount of a good that can be imported.
  • Subsidy: Government support that lowers production costs for certain industries.
  • Trade agreement: An agreement between countries to reduce trade barriers or coordinate trade rules.
  • Protectionism: Policies that protect domestic industries from foreign competition.

International Economic Organizations

  • World Trade Organization: Helps create and enforce rules for international trade.
  • International Monetary Fund: Provides loans and financial assistance to countries facing economic problems.
  • World Bank: Provides loans and support for development projects.
  • Regional trade blocs: Groups of countries that cooperate economically, such as the European Union or USMCA.

Global Supply Chains

A global supply chain is the full network involved in producing and delivering a product. A phone, car, or article of clothing may involve raw materials from one country, parts from another, assembly in another, and sales around the world.

Topic 7.7 — Changes as a Result of the World Economy

🌐 How Globalization Changes Places

The world economy changes where jobs are located, how goods are produced, and which regions gain or lose economic power. Globalization can create growth, but it can also create job loss, uneven development, environmental stress, and dependence on outside markets.

Industrial Location Changes

  • Outsourcing: A company hires another company to complete part of its work.
  • Offshoring: A company moves production or services to another country.
  • Deindustrialization: Decline of manufacturing jobs and industrial activity in a region.
  • Post-Fordism: Flexible production with smaller batches, specialized products, and global supply chains.
  • Automation: Machines and software replace or reduce some types of labor.

Special Economic Zones and Export Processing

  • Special economic zone: An area with special rules, tax breaks, or incentives to attract investment and industry.
  • Export processing zone: A zone focused on producing goods for export.
  • Maquiladora: A factory in Mexico near the U.S. border that often imports parts, assembles products, and exports finished goods.
  • These zones can create jobs and attract foreign investment, but critics argue they may also create low wages, weak labor protections, or environmental problems.

Growth Poles and High-Tech Development

  • Growth pole: A place where economic activity is concentrated and stimulates growth in surrounding areas.
  • Technopole: A high-tech center with research, universities, skilled labor, and technology firms.
  • Agglomeration: Businesses cluster together to share labor, suppliers, infrastructure, and information.
  • Multiplier effect: Economic growth in one industry creates more jobs and spending in related industries.

Impacts of Global Economic Change

  • Older industrial regions may lose factory jobs and population.
  • New industrial regions may gain jobs, infrastructure, and foreign investment.
  • Workers may need new skills as economies shift toward services and technology.
  • Local cultures and economies may become more connected to global corporations and markets.
  • Environmental impacts can increase when production expands quickly without strong regulation.

Topic 7.8 — Sustainable Development

🌱 Development Without Destroying the Future

Sustainable development means improving quality of life while protecting resources, ecosystems, and opportunities for future generations. It tries to balance economic growth, social equity, and environmental protection.

Three Parts of Sustainability

  • Economic sustainability: Creating stable jobs, income, infrastructure, and long-term economic opportunity.
  • Social sustainability: Improving health, education, equality, housing, safety, and human rights.
  • Environmental sustainability: Protecting water, air, soil, biodiversity, climate, and natural resources.

Sustainable Development Strategies

  • Renewable energy: Using solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, and other cleaner energy sources.
  • Energy efficiency: Producing or using goods with less energy waste.
  • Public transportation: Reducing traffic, pollution, and fossil fuel use.
  • Fair trade: Supporting better wages and conditions for producers.
  • Ecotourism: Tourism designed to protect environments and benefit local communities.
  • Local production: Supporting local businesses and reducing long-distance transportation needs.
  • Education and health care: Building human capital for long-term development.

Challenges to Sustainable Development

  • Renewable energy and green infrastructure may require major upfront investment.
  • Poorer regions may need economic growth quickly and may have fewer resources for environmental protection.
  • Global companies may move production to places with weaker regulations.
  • Climate change can damage agriculture, infrastructure, water supplies, and coastal cities.
  • Development can improve living standards but still create inequality if benefits are not shared fairly.

Why Sustainability Matters for APHUG

Sustainability connects almost every unit of AP Human Geography. It links population growth, migration, culture, politics, agriculture, cities, industry, trade, and the environment. On the exam, strong answers often explain both benefits and costs of development.

📝 Multiple Choice Practice

Click an answer to check it. Each question includes an explanation.

1. Which economic sector includes manufacturing and factory production?

(A) Primary sector
(B) Secondary sector
(C) Quaternary sector
(D) Quinary sector
Answer: B. The secondary sector includes manufacturing and processing, such as factory production.

2. Which development measure combines health, education, and income?

(A) GDP only
(B) Human Development Index
(C) Arithmetic density
(D) Rank-size rule
Answer: B. HDI combines life expectancy, education, and income, making it broader than GDP alone.

3. Which theory divides the world economy into core, semi-periphery, and periphery countries?

(A) Von Thünen model
(B) Demographic transition model
(C) World-systems theory
(D) Central place theory
Answer: C. World-systems theory explains global inequality through core, semi-periphery, and periphery relationships.

4. Which is the best example of offshoring?

(A) A company opens another store in the same city.
(B) A company moves manufacturing from its home country to another country.
(C) A farmer rotates crops to improve soil quality.
(D) A government redraws voting districts.
Answer: B. Offshoring means moving production or services to another country.

5. Which strategy best supports sustainable development?

(A) Increasing pollution to lower short-term production costs
(B) Depending completely on one export crop
(C) Expanding renewable energy and improving access to education
(D) Removing all worker protections to attract factories
Answer: C. Sustainable development balances economic growth, social well-being, and environmental protection.

⭐ Key Takeaways

  • Industrialization changed economic geography. Factories, machines, railroads, coal, and cities transformed production and settlement patterns.
  • Economic sectors show development patterns. Economies often shift from primary to secondary to tertiary, quaternary, and quinary activity.
  • Development is more than income. HDI, GII, literacy, life expectancy, infant mortality, and access to services reveal broader conditions.
  • Gender equity supports development. Education, health care, labor rights, land ownership, and political power for women improve economic outcomes.
  • Development theories explain uneven growth. Rostow, dependency theory, and world-systems theory each explain development differently.
  • Trade connects the world economy. Imports, exports, tariffs, quotas, free trade, fair trade, and supply chains shape global connections.
  • Globalization changes job locations. Outsourcing, offshoring, deindustrialization, SEZs, maquiladoras, and technopoles reshape regions.
  • Development creates both benefits and costs. Industrial growth can raise incomes and living standards but also create pollution, inequality, and labor challenges.
  • Sustainable development balances three goals. Strong AP answers explain economic, social, and environmental effects together.
