Unit 6: Cities and Urban Land-Use Patterns and Processes

Urbanization, City Models, Infrastructure, Sustainability, and Urban Change  |  Exam Weight: 12–17%

🏙️ Unit Overview

AP Human Geography Unit 6 focuses on cities and urban land use. Cities are centers of population, culture, transportation, jobs, government, trade, innovation, and inequality. This unit explains why cities form, where they grow, how they are organized, and how urban change affects people and landscapes.

In this unit, you will study urbanization, site and situation, global cities, city networks, urban hierarchy, central place theory, rank-size rule, primate cities, urban models, suburbanization, sprawl, gentrification, infrastructure, sustainability, urban data, and challenges of urban change.

Big question: How do cities develop, organize space, connect to globalization, and create both opportunities and challenges?

🗓️ Unit 6 Concept Timeline

Ancient Era: Early cities develop in river valleys and trade centers, often near fertile land, water, and transportation routes.
Classical Era: Cities such as Rome, Athens, Chang’an, and Teotihuacán become centers of government, trade, culture, and religion.
Medieval Era: Walled cities, market towns, religious centers, and trade cities grow across Europe, Asia, Africa, and the Middle East.
1500s–1800s: Colonialism creates port cities and administrative capitals that connect colonies to global trade networks.
1800s: Industrialization causes rapid urbanization as factories attract rural migrants to cities.
1900s: Automobiles, highways, suburbanization, zoning, and mass transit reshape urban land use.
Late 1900s: Globalization strengthens world cities as centers of finance, culture, technology, and corporate control.
Today: Cities face challenges involving housing, sprawl, transportation, segregation, sustainability, climate risk, infrastructure, and inequality.

Topic 6.1 — The Origin and Influences of Urbanization

🌆 Why Cities Begin and Grow

Urbanization is the growth in the percentage of people living in cities. Cities usually grow because they offer jobs, services, transportation, education, markets, government functions, and cultural opportunities.

Basic Urban Terms

  • City: A large, densely populated settlement with many economic, political, and cultural functions.
  • Urban area: A built-up area with high population density and non-agricultural land use.
  • Urbanization: The process of people moving to cities and cities expanding.
  • Urbanism: The way of life associated with living in cities.
  • Metropolitan area: A city and the surrounding suburbs and communities connected to it economically and socially.

Site and Situation

  • Site: The physical characteristics of a city’s location, such as climate, landforms, water, soil, and elevation.
  • Situation: A city’s location relative to other places, trade routes, resources, and transportation networks.
  • Example: A port city may grow because its site provides a harbor and its situation connects it to global trade routes.

Reasons Cities Grow

  • Economic opportunity: Factories, offices, ports, tourism, finance, and services attract workers.
  • Transportation: Roads, railroads, airports, rivers, and ports connect cities to other places.
  • Government: Capitals and administrative centers attract people and institutions.
  • Education and health care: Universities and hospitals create jobs and draw residents.
  • Culture: Museums, restaurants, sports, entertainment, and diverse communities attract people.

Topic 6.2 — Cities Across the World

🌍 Urbanization Looks Different by Region

Cities exist in every world region, but they do not all grow in the same way. Some cities grew from ancient trade and religious centers. Others grew from colonialism, industrialization, migration, or globalization.

Different Types of Cities

  • Megacity: A metropolitan area with more than 10 million people.
  • Metacity: A city with more than 20 million people.
  • Primate city: A city that is much larger and more important than any other city in the same country.
  • Global city: A city that strongly influences the global economy, culture, politics, and transportation networks.
  • Colonial city: A city shaped by imperial powers, often with ports, administrative districts, and European-style planning.

Urbanization in Developed Countries

  • Urbanization happened earlier, especially during industrialization.
  • Many cities now grow slowly or even lose population in their central areas.
  • Suburbanization, redevelopment, and gentrification are common urban processes.
  • Infrastructure is usually more developed, but aging roads, bridges, housing, and transit can still be major problems.

Urbanization in Developing Countries

  • Urbanization is often faster and more recent.
  • Rural-to-urban migration can cause rapid city growth.
  • Governments may struggle to provide housing, sanitation, transportation, schools, and health care fast enough.
  • Informal settlements may grow when formal housing is too expensive or unavailable.

Topic 6.3 — Cities and Globalization

🌐 Cities as Global Nodes

Globalization connects cities through trade, finance, transportation, communication, migration, media, and technology. Some cities become powerful global nodes because they control flows of money, information, goods, and people.

Characteristics of Global Cities

  • Major financial institutions, stock exchanges, banks, and corporate headquarters.
  • Advanced transportation links such as international airports, seaports, rail networks, and highways.
  • Concentrations of producer services such as law, advertising, consulting, design, accounting, and technology.
  • Cultural influence through media, fashion, universities, museums, tourism, sports, and entertainment.
  • Large immigrant communities and strong connections to other world regions.

Global City Examples

  • New York: Finance, media, culture, diplomacy, immigration, and corporate power.
  • London: Finance, transportation, global business, and colonial historical connections.
  • Tokyo: Technology, finance, manufacturing command, transportation, and high-density urban life.
  • Singapore: Port activity, finance, trade, and strategic location in Southeast Asia.

Effects of Globalization on Cities

  • Creates jobs in finance, technology, tourism, logistics, and services.
  • Increases cultural diversity through migration and global media.
  • Raises land values in central areas and can increase housing costs.
  • Can deepen inequality between wealthy districts and low-income neighborhoods.

Topic 6.4 — The Size and Distribution of Cities

📍 Why Cities Are Arranged in Patterns

Cities are not randomly distributed. They form networks and hierarchies based on size, function, location, transportation, history, resources, and economic power.

Urban Hierarchy

  • Urban hierarchy: Ranking cities based on size, influence, and services.
  • Hamlet or village: Small settlement with limited services.
  • Town: Larger settlement with more services and businesses.
  • City: Larger urban area with many specialized functions.
  • Metropolis: Large city with suburbs and strong regional influence.
  • Global city: City with major worldwide influence.

Central Place Theory

  • Central place: A settlement that provides goods and services to surrounding areas.
  • Hinterland: The surrounding area served by a central place.
  • Threshold: The minimum number of customers needed to support a business or service.
  • Range: The maximum distance people are willing to travel for a good or service.
  • High-order goods: Specialized goods or services people travel farther to get, such as hospitals or universities.
  • Low-order goods: Everyday goods people buy nearby, such as groceries or gas.

Rank-Size Rule and Primate Cities

  • Rank-size rule: The nth largest city is about 1/n the size of the largest city.
  • Primate city: The largest city is much bigger and more influential than the next largest cities.
  • Meaning: Rank-size distribution suggests a more balanced urban system, while primacy suggests strong concentration of power in one city.

Topic 6.5 — The Internal Structure of Cities

🏙️ How Cities Are Organized Inside

Urban models help geographers explain patterns inside cities. These models are simplified, so they are not perfect, but they help explain residential zones, business districts, transportation corridors, industry, suburbs, and inequality.

Classic North American Urban Models

  • Burgess concentric zone model: City grows outward in rings from the central business district.
  • Hoyt sector model: City grows in wedge-shaped sectors along transportation routes.
  • Harris and Ullman multiple nuclei model: City has several centers or nodes, not just one downtown.
  • Galactic city model: City is spread out with edge cities, suburbs, highways, and decentralized activity.
  • Bid-rent theory: Land value is highest near the center, so land users compete based on what they can pay.

Models from Other World Regions

  • Latin American city model: Often includes a strong central business district, elite residential spine, and peripheral informal settlements.
  • Southeast Asian city model: Often includes port zones, market gardening areas, mixed land use, and colonial influences.
  • African city model: Often includes colonial CBDs, traditional markets, ethnic neighborhoods, and informal settlements.

Model Limitations

  • Models simplify real cities and may not fit every place.
  • Historical, cultural, political, and environmental factors change urban structure.
  • Transportation technology, zoning laws, globalization, and government policy can reshape land use.
  • Models may not fully explain informal settlements, inequality, or rapid urban growth in developing regions.

Topic 6.6 — Density and Land Use

🏘️ How Urban Land Is Used

Urban land use shows how different parts of a city are organized for housing, business, industry, transportation, recreation, government, and public services.

Common Urban Land Uses

  • Central business district: The downtown core with offices, stores, government buildings, entertainment, and high land values.
  • Residential land use: Areas where people live, including apartments, townhouses, single-family homes, and high-rises.
  • Commercial land use: Stores, restaurants, hotels, offices, malls, and business districts.
  • Industrial land use: Factories, warehouses, shipping facilities, and manufacturing areas.
  • Mixed-use development: Combines housing, shopping, offices, and recreation in one area.
  • Public land use: Schools, libraries, parks, government buildings, roads, and transit facilities.

Housing Density

  • Low-density housing: Single-family homes, larger lots, and automobile dependence.
  • Medium-density housing: Townhouses, duplexes, small apartment buildings, and walkable neighborhoods.
  • High-density housing: Large apartment buildings, high-rises, and compact urban living.

Zoning and Land Use

  • Zoning: Government rules that determine how land can be used.
  • Zoning can separate residential, commercial, industrial, and public land uses.
  • Zoning can protect neighborhoods but can also increase segregation or limit affordable housing.
  • Infill development: Building on empty or underused land inside existing urban areas.
  • Brownfield: Abandoned or polluted industrial land that can be cleaned and reused.

Topic 6.7 — Infrastructure

🚇 The Systems That Keep Cities Running

Infrastructure includes the basic systems and services that allow a city to function. The quality and location of infrastructure strongly affect economic opportunity, public health, mobility, and inequality.

Types of Urban Infrastructure

  • Transportation: Roads, bridges, highways, buses, trains, subways, sidewalks, bike lanes, airports, and ports.
  • Utilities: Water, electricity, natural gas, sewage, internet, and waste collection.
  • Public services: Schools, hospitals, libraries, parks, police stations, fire stations, and government offices.
  • Green infrastructure: Parks, trees, wetlands, green roofs, rain gardens, and systems that manage stormwater naturally.

Infrastructure and Inequality

  • Wealthier neighborhoods often have better roads, parks, schools, transit access, and public services.
  • Low-income neighborhoods may face fewer transit options, older infrastructure, pollution, or limited access to healthy food.
  • Poor infrastructure can make it harder for people to reach jobs, schools, health care, and grocery stores.
  • Infrastructure investment can improve quality of life but can also raise property values and contribute to displacement.

Topic 6.8 — Urban Sustainability

🌱 Making Cities More Livable

Urban sustainability means designing cities to meet current needs while protecting resources and quality of life for the future. Sustainable cities try to reduce pollution, preserve land, improve transportation, and provide fair access to services.

Sustainable Urban Strategies

  • Smart growth: Planning that limits sprawl and encourages compact, mixed-use development.
  • New Urbanism: Design movement that promotes walkable neighborhoods, mixed land use, public spaces, and less car dependence.
  • Transit-oriented development: Building housing, jobs, and services near public transportation.
  • Greenbelts: Protected open land around cities that limits outward growth.
  • Urban infill: Developing empty or underused land inside existing cities.
  • Green roofs: Roofs with vegetation that can reduce heat, manage stormwater, and improve insulation.

Benefits

  • Reduces traffic congestion and car dependence.
  • Protects farmland, forests, and open space outside cities.
  • Improves air quality and reduces greenhouse gas emissions.
  • Creates walkable neighborhoods with access to services.
  • Can reduce flooding and urban heat island effects.

Challenges

  • Sustainable development can be expensive.
  • New transit or green improvements may raise property values.
  • Residents may disagree about density, zoning, parking, and development.
  • Low-income communities may not receive equal access to sustainability projects.

Topic 6.9 — Urban Data

📊 Using Data to Understand Cities

Urban geographers use data to study housing, transportation, income, race and ethnicity, pollution, health, land use, population density, business activity, infrastructure, and access to services.

Types of Urban Data

  • Census data: Population information about age, income, household size, housing, and other traits.
  • GIS data: Digital layers that show roads, buildings, land use, zoning, transit, schools, and environmental patterns.
  • Remote sensing: Satellite or aerial images used to study land cover, sprawl, heat islands, and urban growth.
  • Transportation data: Traffic counts, commute times, transit ridership, and pedestrian movement.
  • Housing data: Rent prices, home values, vacancies, evictions, and building permits.

How Urban Data Is Used

  • Identify neighborhoods with limited access to grocery stores, parks, schools, or transit.
  • Plan bus routes, bike lanes, sidewalks, housing, and public services.
  • Map pollution exposure and environmental justice issues.
  • Study gentrification, displacement, suburbanization, and segregation.
  • Prepare for flooding, heat waves, wildfires, and other climate risks.

Limitations and Privacy

Urban data can be useful, but it can also be incomplete, outdated, biased, or too broad to show neighborhood-level differences. Location data can also raise privacy concerns when it tracks people’s movement.

Topic 6.10 — Challenges of Urban Change

⚠️ Urban Growth Creates Problems and Opportunities

Cities constantly change. Some changes create jobs, housing, transportation, and cultural growth. Other changes create inequality, displacement, environmental stress, and political conflict.

Suburbanization and Sprawl

  • Suburbanization: Growth of areas outside the central city.
  • Urban sprawl: Low-density, car-dependent development spreading outward from a city.
  • Edge city: A suburban area with offices, shopping, entertainment, and jobs outside the traditional downtown.
  • Exurb: A settlement beyond the suburbs, often connected to the city by commuting.
  • Boomburb: A rapidly growing suburban city.

Problems Linked to Sprawl

  • Loss of farmland and natural habitats.
  • Longer commutes and traffic congestion.
  • Higher infrastructure costs for roads, utilities, and schools.
  • Greater car dependence and air pollution.
  • Social separation between income groups.

Gentrification and Displacement

  • Gentrification: Wealthier residents and businesses move into a lower-income urban area, often raising property values and rents.
  • Positive effects: New investment, renovated buildings, businesses, services, and tax revenue.
  • Negative effects: Higher rents, cultural change, and displacement of long-time residents.

Segregation and Uneven Development

  • Redlining: Historical denial of loans or investment in neighborhoods based on race or ethnicity.
  • Blockbusting: Real estate practice that encouraged fear-based selling and racial turnover in neighborhoods.
  • Disamenity zone: Area with poverty, weak infrastructure, informal housing, or environmental risk.
  • Environmental justice: Fair treatment of all communities regarding pollution, land use, and environmental protection.

Informal Settlements

Informal settlements grow when people need housing but cannot access formal housing markets. These areas may lack secure land ownership, clean water, sanitation, electricity, paved roads, or public services. They often reflect rapid urbanization and economic inequality.

📝 Multiple Choice Practice

Click an answer to check it. Each question includes an explanation.

1. Which term refers to the physical characteristics of a city’s location?

(A) Site
(B) Situation
(C) Range
(D) Threshold
Answer: A. Site means the physical characteristics of a location, such as landforms, water, elevation, and climate.

2. Which concept explains the minimum number of customers needed to support a service?

(A) Range
(B) Threshold
(C) Sprawl
(D) Bid rent
Answer: B. Threshold is the minimum market size needed to support a business or service.

3. Which urban model describes a city with several centers of activity instead of one single downtown core?

(A) Concentric zone model
(B) Von Thünen model
(C) Multiple nuclei model
(D) Demographic transition model
Answer: C. The multiple nuclei model argues that cities have several nodes of activity, such as downtowns, shopping centers, airports, and industrial districts.

4. Which is the best example of urban sprawl?

(A) Compact apartments built near a subway station
(B) Low-density suburbs spreading outward along highways
(C) A downtown financial district with high-rise buildings
(D) A small village surrounded by farmland
Answer: B. Urban sprawl is low-density, car-dependent development that spreads outward from a city.

5. Which urban planning strategy is most closely connected to smart growth?

(A) Expanding highways into farmland
(B) Building only single-family homes far from jobs
(C) Creating walkable, mixed-use neighborhoods near public transit
(D) Removing all public transportation from the city center
Answer: C. Smart growth promotes compact, walkable, mixed-use development and better access to public transportation.

⭐ Key Takeaways

  • Cities grow because of site, situation, and opportunity. Jobs, transportation, trade, services, and government functions attract people.
  • Urbanization varies by region. Developed countries urbanized earlier, while many developing countries are urbanizing quickly today.
  • Global cities are powerful nodes. They control flows of finance, information, transportation, culture, and corporate decision-making.
  • City systems have patterns. Urban hierarchy, central place theory, rank-size rule, and primate cities explain city size and distribution.
  • Urban models simplify city structure. Concentric zone, sector, multiple nuclei, galactic, and regional models help explain land use.
  • Land use reflects power and values. Zoning, density, housing, CBDs, transportation, and infrastructure shape urban landscapes.
  • Infrastructure affects opportunity. Transit, roads, utilities, schools, parks, and health care influence economic and social development.
  • Urban sustainability tries to reduce harm. Smart growth, New Urbanism, transit-oriented development, infill, and greenbelts fight sprawl.
  • Urban change creates winners and losers. Gentrification, suburbanization, segregation, redlining, and informal settlements show how cities can produce inequality.
